
\begin{abstract}
Axelrod's work proved that cooperation emerges mechanistically through repeated interaction. Since then, the field evolved to study emergent mechanisms in multi-agent RL and LLMs. This thesis asks whether LLMs, trained on human language and cultural patterns, enable qualitatively different emergent structures by comparing self-optimizing RL agents with self-optimizing LLM agents in a novel coordination game, measuring cooperation, coalitions, hierarchy and conflict. 

If LLMs emerge different dynamics, it suggest LLMs enable coordination that pure optimization cannot.
\end{abstract}
